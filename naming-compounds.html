Why covalent compounds need prefixes at all

Two metals-and-nonmetals only ever combine one way, because the charges have to cancel. Two nonmetals share electrons instead, and they can share in more than one arrangement. So the name has to carry the count — otherwise these two compounds would have the same name and one of them will kill you.
Compare that to ionic. There is only one magnesium chloride, and it is MgCl2. Magnesium is always 2+, chlorine is always 1−, so the formula has no choice. That is exactly why ionic names never use prefixes — the name does not need to carry a count, because the charges already fix it.

The seven diatomic elements

These seven never appear alone. Left by themselves they pair up with a twin. That is what diatomic means — two atoms of the same element. It does not mean a two-element compound, which is what everything else on this page is.
Memory trick: Br I N Cl H O F — "Brinklehof." Or find them on the periodic table: they make a 7 shape, starting at hydrogen and running down the halogen column.
Careful: O2 is oxygen gas, an element. CO2 is carbon dioxide, a compound. A subscript alone does not make something a compound — two different symbols do.
